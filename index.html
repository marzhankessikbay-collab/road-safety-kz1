Kazakhstan · Road traffic deaths
6/ day

About six people die on Kazakhstan's roads every day.

What if AI watched the road instead of the driver?

Why the numbers hide as much as they show, what AI could really do, what it would cost, and a verdict at the end of the road.

Marzhan Kessikbay · PLS 419/519 Final project · 2026
1 KM

The Problem: a death toll that stays high

Road crashes kill thousands of people in Kazakhstan every year, most of them of working age. In 2024, 2,579 people died on the roads, and the death rate is about five times Germany's.1,2 Behind each number is a family, and the deaths are not spread evenly: they cluster in certain regions, on certain roads, and among certain people.

Who are they? Mostly young men and people on foot. Two-thirds die at the scene, which points to slow emergency help, not only to bad driving. And because many victims are their family's main earner, one crash can push a whole household into poverty.

Something strange happened between 2018 and 2022: recorded crashes went down, but deaths went up. Crashes were becoming more deadly. Deaths dipped to about 2,300 in 2025,2 but one good year does not make a trend.

My question: Kazakhstan has spent heavily on AI traffic cameras. Why are deaths still so high, and who pays? My answer: road safety is treated as a policing problem (catch drivers, collect fines) instead of a roads and health problem. Those who pay have the least say over how roads are built: pedestrians, rural drivers far from hospitals, and young working men.
2 KM

Data: what the numbers show, and what they hide

Before trusting any number, I wanted to know where it comes from. Crash data in Kazakhstan is collected by several institutions that don't talk to each other: police, hospitals, insurers and the statistics office. Each one sees a different part of the picture, and some crashes fall through the gaps between them.

What I trust: the number of deaths, to show the general direction. What I don't trust: year-to-year comparisons of crashes and injuries, and official comparisons with neighbouring countries. This matters for my solution: any AI trained on these records will inherit their blind spots.

What the data cannot see

🦽Serious injuriesThere is no legal definition, so people disabled by crashes are invisible.3
📍Exact locationsThere is no GPS, so dangerous spots are known only roughly.3
🚗How much people driveWithout it, nobody can say which region is riskier per trip.3
🏘️VillagesRural victims and crashes settled without police slip through.
3 KM

Research: five ways to look at the same road

Researchers study road deaths in very different ways. Each approach sees a different problem, and so suggests a different fix. I grouped the academic literature into five approaches, from "who dies" to "who holds power".

1
Doctors and health researchers ask: who dies?They studied death records and found that most victims are men, many are pedestrians, and most die right at the crash site. What's missing: they show the problem, but not how to fix it.Myssayev et al. 2014; Akbar et al. 20264,5
2
Economists ask: does it get better as a country gets richer?They found that when a country gets richer, more people buy cars, so road deaths go up first. Only later, when the country is richer still, do deaths start to fall. Kazakhstan may still be in this "going up" stage. Open question: do we just wait, or can good policy make deaths fall sooner?Kopits & Cropper 20058
3
Traffic researchers ask: do speed cameras work?Yes, they reduce crashes near the camera. But drivers learn where the cameras are: they slow down at the camera and speed up again after it (the two charts below show this). What's missing: nobody independent has checked whether Kazakhstan's AI cameras work.Wilson et al. 2010; De Pauw et al. 20149,23
4
Data scientists ask: can AI predict where crashes will happen?Yes, computer programs can learn from past crashes to predict future ones. The catch: they need exact crash locations and police and hospital records joined together, and Kazakhstan doesn't have that yet.Silva et al. 2020; Ali et al. 202410,11
5
Political scientists ask: who controls the cameras?They studied camera systems in Almaty, Kyiv and Bishkek. Governments install them to look modern and keep order, but the public has little say over how they are used. What's missing: they don't check whether the cameras actually made roads safer.Marat & Sutton 202112

Put together, the research says cameras can reduce crashes, but drivers quickly learn where they are. I see this myself in Astana: drivers know which parts of the bus lanes Sergek doesn't cover, and they break the rules exactly there. A camera teaches drivers where to behave, not how to drive.

The gap: nobody has asked what AI on Kazakhstan's roads would look like if it were built to prevent deaths and judged by lives saved, not by fines collected.
4 KM

The Solution: Jol Radar

AI is already on Kazakhstan's roads, and it is pointed at drivers: highway cameras that spot animals and read plates,27 and Astana cameras that catch phones and seatbelts.28 I read what drivers said about it:

"You'd do better to learn how to build roads properly."
"So they recognise it, and then what? They can only write a fine."
"How will this help, if the services are 40 km away?"
"A deer ran out. So what? What use is that to the driver?"
Today
📷 → 🚗 driver → 🧾 fine

AI watches the driver.
Drivers learn where cameras are, and hide the phone below the camera's view.

Jol Radar
📱 on a bus → 🕳️ road → 🛠️ repair

AI watches the road.
Every detection leads to something a driver can feel.

Jol Radar ("road radar") turns the AI around. Instead of catching drivers, it finds what is wrong with the road and makes sure someone fixes it. It doesn't need new cameras: it uses phones on vehicles that already drive every road, plus the complaints people already send. Each of the five steps uses a different kind of AI:

1Scancomputer visionPhones on buses and post vans film the road; AI marks potholes, cracks and broken lights.
2Listenlanguage modelAI reads complaints in Kazakh and Russian (eOtinish, 1403) and puts them on the same map.
3Rank & fixrisk predictionThe most dangerous defects get fixed first, each with a public deadline. No fines for swerving around an unfixed pothole.
4Alerthazard detectionPotholes, ice and animals go to 2GIS and Yandex Navigator. Hazards only, never plates.
5Place helphotspot mappingIt shows where crashes cluster and ambulances arrive slowest, so we know where rescue posts belong.

Prerequisite: GPS crash forms linked to hospital records.3 Run by KazAvtoZhol and the Ministries of Transport and Health. Pilot in the Almaty region, which has the most deaths and an existing AI pilot on the Almaty–Konaev road.37

The repair step is the heart of the plan. Kazakhstan already finds thousands of defects every year, but only about half get fixed. Finding more potholes only helps if the money and the deadlines to fix them follow.

It already works elsewhere

Each step is based on something that has already been tried somewhere else, including one warning about what can go wrong:

−17%Las VegasAI predicted crash spots, police and signs acted, crashes fell.13→ steps 3–4
−40%Telangana, IndiaAI on 200 buses flagged danger spots; their crashes fell.16→ steps 3, 5
4,200 kmDetroit, USAOne phone on a windscreen plus AI checked every road in the city.31→ step 1
500+GermanyTowns using vialytics, an app that finds potholes from phones in city cars.32→ step 1
⚠Boston, USAStreet Bump relied on volunteers' phones and missed poorer areas.33,34→ so use buses, not volunteers
44 bn ₸Sergek, AlmatyFines collected. It claims 40% fewer deaths (not independently checked) and once fined drivers for swerving around potholes.17,26

The bill: what it would cost

Every AI solution has a price, and not only in money. I priced Jol Radar in five ways: the environment, the economy, workers, politics and data. The surprising result is that the AI itself is the cheapest part.

JOL RADAR · TOLL RECEIPTPayer: the public

🌍 EnvironmentLOW

Does it harm nature? Not much. Jol Radar adds no new cameras; it uses phones that are already on buses. That matters because 74.9% of Kazakhstan's electricity comes from coal,20 so every new machine means more coal burned. Any new roadside equipment should run on solar power, like KazAvtoZhol's new cameras.27

💰 MoneyHIGH

Is it expensive? The AI itself is cheap. Fixing the roads is not: the state plans to spend 1.2 trillion ₸ on roads, but they need 1.9 trillion.2 Rescue posts also mean paying staff. Who could make money? Tech companies, and cities, if the system is turned into a machine for fines.17,30

👷 WorkersMEDIUM

Whose work changes? Behind every AI there are people checking its work: labelling photos and fixing its mistakes. This is called "ghost work", and it is often badly paid.21 These people must be paid fairly. Bus drivers should be told and asked before phones go on their buses.

🏛️ Power & controlHIGH

Could it be misused? Yes. Sergek was built for traffic, but during COVID-19 it was used to track people.18 Cameras that read licence plates help with safety, for example checking average speed between two points,29 but they also show where every car went. And Astana's new camera system is being built by a company from the UAE,30 so who keeps the data is a real question.

🔐 Data & privacyHIGH

Is people's data safe? Hospital records are private health data, so names must be removed. Road videos can show people and car plates, so these must be blurred on the phone before they're sent. The biggest danger: if the AI learns only from police data, it may ignore villages, because crashes there are recorded less often.
TOTALThe AI is the cheapest line on this bill.

The biggest risks are political, not technical. A system built for roads could slowly be used to watch people, as happened with Sergek during COVID-19. And if the AI learns only from police data, it will send money to well-recorded city streets and away from villages, where people actually die.

Verdict: should Kazakhstan do this?

ONLY UNDER CONDITIONS
Not red, not green. Amber: proceed only if all of these are met.

Jol Radar could save lives, but only if it is built the right way. Built on today's data and run by the institutions that collect fines, it would drift toward revenue and surveillance. That's why my answer is not yes or no, but amber: proceed only if…

✓ Watch the road, not the driver
✓ Fix the data first: GPS forms + police–hospital link
✓ A 2-year pilot in the Almaty region, checked by a university
✓ Repair money follows the map
✕ No faces, no plates, no driver scores
✕ The AI company is never paid from fine money

Speeding fines stay. Police and existing cameras keep doing their job. Jol Radar just doesn't hand out fines itself, so nobody profits from catching more drivers instead of saving lives.

If these can't be met: skip the AI and spend the money on roads, lights and ambulances. Drivers keep saying it: the road is the problem.
